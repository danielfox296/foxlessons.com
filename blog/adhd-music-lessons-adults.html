If music lessons never stuck, it was the method

You had lessons and quit. You taught yourself and stalled. You own the instrument and it's a shelf. None of that is a character flaw, and here's what's different.

Daniel Fox Denver · 2026-10-26 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • If lessons didn't stick before, look at the method before you look at yourself. The standard one is built on obligation, and ADHD runs on interest.
  • Wanting structure and wanting to play your own music aren't opposites. The structure gets built around a goal you named.
  • You don't need a diagnosis, you don't need to be a beginner, and you don't need to read music.

Most adults with ADHD who come to me have already tried. Lessons as a kid that ended the day the practice log came up empty one too many times. Years of YouTube and tabs that got you to a certain level and then stopped moving. A guitar bought with real intention, now holding up a houseplant. By the time you’re reading this, you’ve probably got a story about being someone who quits.

I don’t think that story is true. I think you got handed a method built for somebody else’s attention, and when it failed, everyone agreed the problem was you. Here’s what that method got wrong for each of those three people, and what the first month looks like instead.

You had lessons and quit #

Picture the lesson you had at eleven. A method book. A song you didn’t pick and didn’t care about. Thirty minutes a day, logged. Notation before you were allowed to make a sound that felt like music. And each week, the small ceremony of the teacher asking how practice went and you learning to lie about it.

That’s a method built on obligation, and it works for a certain kind of attention. Yours isn’t that kind. Interest is what keeps an ADHD brain on a task, and that method spent your interest on week one and never put any back.

What’s different: we start with music you’re already listening to, in the first lesson, before any of the scaffolding. If an exercise is needed, it’s because it unlocks a song you want, and it goes away when the song works. There is no practice log. If a week went blank, we don’t do the ceremony. We figure out what got in the way, which is usually something physical, and we fix that.

You taught yourself and stalled #

This is the one I hear most from adults, and it’s the most frustrating, because you did the hard part. You got yourself to the instrument, alone, for years. You can play. And now you keep making the same mistakes in the same places, the songs you like aren’t making you better anymore, and nobody’s watching to tell you why.

Here’s the trap. A lot of what’s online says ADHD players should just play what they love, and you did, and it stopped working. So you go looking for the opposite: someone strict, someone who’ll hold you accountable, drills, theory, the serious version. And you brace yourself for lessons that feel like the ones you quit.

You don’t have to pick a side. Interest-first means the songs are yours. It doesn’t mean nobody pushes. The feedback matches what you’ve told me you’re committed to: if you’re in it for fun it stays light, and if you’ve named something specific, a gig, a jam, a set, a date, I’ll coach you hard toward it. The theory and the technique come in as far as the songs you want need them, which, at your level, is usually further than you’d think. And the ear you built teaching yourself is an asset. We use it instead of replacing it with paper.

You own the instrument and it's a shelf #

You bought it. You meant it. You played it for two weeks and then it went in the case, the case went in the closet, and now it’s been long enough that opening the closet feels like an accusation.

Usually the wall is starting, not playing. The ten seconds of friction between wanting to play and playing is where the whole evening goes, and ADHD makes that friction enormous. The instrument isn’t the problem and neither are you. The closet is.

What’s different: the first thing we do is get the instrument out of its case and into the room where you already sit, in your way. That alone fixes more than any practice plan. Then we make the first thing you play when you pick it up something that sounds good immediately, because the reward has to land before your attention leaves. Practice goes for a feeling, not a number. If you get the lick in five minutes, you’re done, and done counts.

What the first month actually looks like #

The first lesson is a real lesson. We play, we talk, and I map where you are and what you’re into. You leave with something to work on that fits your hands and your goal, and you pay after, only if it was worth it.

Lessons are in your home in the Denver metro or on Zoom, an hour, held at the same time every week. Inside the hour we switch when the energy drops: songs, instruments, ear work, hands. Music is physical and mental at once, and moving between those is how we reset. Lights, volume, breaks and the room get set up around you. Midweek, if you want it, I check in by text: did you get to it, where did it snag, quick answers on the tune.

You don’t need a diagnosis or paperwork for any of this. It’s how I teach everyone. The ADHD page has the full method, and the practice post goes deeper on what a session should look like once you’re rolling.

I’m a music teacher, not a music therapist. My job is helping you play. If you’re looking for clinical goals with music, Denver has board-certified music therapists, and I’m glad to point you toward them. If you’re weighing the two, music lessons or music therapy lays out the line.
