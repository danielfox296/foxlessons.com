How to practice guitar when you have ADHD

Skip the discipline lectures. Practice that works for an ADHD player runs on interest, short varied reps, and a guitar that's impossible to ignore.

Daniel Fox Denver · 2026-08-03 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • Play music you actually care about. Interest is what keeps an ADHD player playing, and self-chosen songs are the strongest predictor of sticking with it in the research on everyone.
  • Go for the feel of a thing and don't count minutes. If you get the lick down in five minutes, you're done with it.
  • Starting is usually the wall, so design for it. Keep the guitar out of the case, in the room where you already sit.

Practicing guitar with ADHD is a design problem. Sessions built on interest, variety, and quick wins work, and minute quotas, assigned songs and shame don’t. Here is how I set practice up with my students, and how to set it up for yourself.

Why is practicing guitar so hard with ADHD? #

Usually the wall is starting, because most ADHD players care intensely about music. The guitar is in its case, the case is in another room, and the ten seconds of friction between wanting to play and playing is where the whole evening goes.

Then the guilt makes it worse. A blank week turns into a story about being someone who quits, you open the case less, and by week three the guitar is a monument to self-blame. That’s what happens when practice is designed for someone else’s attention.

What should a practice session look like? #

It should be short, varied, and yours. We work on real songs you’re actually interested in, immediately, and I bring in exercises only as far as they serve a song you want to play.

Inside a session, switching is allowed. We can change songs. We can switch what we’re working on entirely if that’s what resets you. I only push on something when you’re close to a breakthrough. Otherwise we follow whatever’s interesting. Music is physical, so moving, standing up, and playing something loud for a minute are all normal parts of practice.

And the target is never a timer.

How do I start when I can't start? #

Put the guitar where you sit, out of the case, on a stand, in the middle of the room if that’s what it takes. A lot of times that alone does it, because the ten seconds of friction are gone and the instrument is right there.

If it still isn’t happening, the fix is coaching rather than willpower. We figure out what pulls you toward the guitar and what you’re afraid of, and we work with both. After a blank week there’s no shame ritual. We problem-solve what got in the way, reconnect the playing to what you actually care about, and start again. Restarts are the norm, and we plan for them.

Do apps help or hurt? #

Both. Play-along apps are good at getting you to show up, and the streak mechanics help a lot of ADHD players, so keep the app.

Here is what I noticed after years of playing along with records: playing along feels really good, but you don’t necessarily critique yourself. When the headphones are on and the band sounds wonderful, you are captured by the emotion of it, and your own technique rides along unexamined. That’s the app’s blind spot too, since it can see your notes and can’t see your hands. Pair the app with a teacher who watches you play and you get both halves. I wrote a fuller comparison in apps versus a teacher.

Do I need a diagnosis to ask for lessons like this? #

No. You don’t need a diagnosis to want lessons that work this way. This is simply how I teach: interest first, movement normal, feedback calibrated to your goals. Founding students also get direct text access between lessons and a midweek check-in by text or call, for accountability and hard spots.

I’m a music teacher, not a music therapist. My job is helping you play. If you’re looking for clinical goals with music, Denver has board-certified music therapists, and I’m glad to point you toward them. Everything on the ADHD lessons page describes teaching, nothing more.
