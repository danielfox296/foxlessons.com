Jams · Blues

How blues jams work

Blues jams are the easiest jams to walk into. The songs follow a short twelve-bar form that most players already know, so a good host can put a new player into a band within one tune.

What happens on the night

What gets played

The night is mostly twelve-bar blues in E, A, G and C, the keys guitarists like, played as shuffles and slow blues. Bands also play a handful of standards most players can fake, like Stormy Monday, The Thrill Is Gone and Sweet Home Chicago, and someone usually calls a minor blues or a funk blues.

How signup works

Most blues jams keep a paper list or a whiteboard near the stage. The host builds small bands from the list and calls players up in groups, mixing instruments so every group has drums, bass and someone playing chords. Signup times and methods vary from night to night.

How a set goes

Each group usually plays two or three tunes. The player who calls a tune names the key, counts it in and leads it. Solos go around the band once, the leader signals the last time through, and the band finds the ending together.

Habits worth knowing

  • Keep a solo to one or two times through the form unless the leader waves you on.
  • Play softer than you think you need to behind a singer or another soloist.
  • Learn the quick change, the jump to the IV chord in bar two, because hosts call it without warning.
  • If you don't know a tune, say so before it starts, since the whole band stalls when one player is lost in the form.

What each instrumentalist needs

Find your instrument, then the level you want to play at.

  • 1 to 2, first-timer friendly Your first time on any stage is fine, and so is playing basic chords or a groove and following along.
  • 3, middle You hold your part, take a solo or a verse, and know the common tunes.
  • 4 to 5, experienced People expect you to know the tunes by name and keep up, and working pros sit in.

All five levels, defined

Guitarists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play barre or open chords through a twelve-bar blues in E and A with a steady shuffle strum, and you stay out of the singer's way.
3, middle
You know the quick change and the common turnarounds, you can voice seventh and ninth chords, and you can take a solo with the minor pentatonic scale and trade solos with another guitarist.
4 to 5, experienced
You can phrase a slow blues, catch stops and hits, play in any key that gets called, comp under a soloist without crowding them, and lead a band through an ending.

Bassists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can hold the roots through a twelve-bar blues in E and A while you watch the guitarist for the chord changes.
3, middle
You can play shuffle and walking lines in the common keys, handle the quick change, and follow the band through turnarounds and endings.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play in any key that gets called, handle a minor blues and stops and hits, lay back under a singer, and take a solo.

We learn the forms and keys that get called, and we practice following a bandleader you've never played with. Bass lessons

Drummers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play a steady shuffle and a slow 12/8 feel at one volume, and keep the tempo the leader counted in.
3, middle
You can move between a shuffle, a slow blues, a two-feel and a funk backbeat, and set up the stops and the last time through.
4 to 5, experienced
You can follow a singer's dynamics, catch hits and stop-time on the fly, play train and rhumba grooves, and end a tune cleanly off the leader's signal.

We drill the three or four grooves blues jams call, and we practice reading a leader you just met. Drum lessons

Keyboard players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play simple triads or seventh chords through a twelve-bar blues in C, G and E, keep it light, and keep your left hand out of the bass player's range.
3, middle
You can comp a shuffle, play a slow blues with a gospel feel, play organ pads behind solos, and take a solo of your own.
4 to 5, experienced
You know blues piano and organ language, you can play an intro or an ending on request, and you comp lightly enough that the soloist stays out front.

We build chord-chart fluency in the jam keys, then practice comping under a singer. Piano lessons

Singers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You know one tune all the way through, in a key you've sung it in, and you tell the band that key before the count-in.
3, middle
You have two or three tunes in your keys, you can cue the band for stops and the last time through, and you can work a mic and PA you've never used.
4 to 5, experienced
You can sing a set's worth of material, change keys on request, direct the band with hand signals, and pull a crowd's attention back from the bar.

Horn players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You know the twelve-bar form by ear, you count bars so you come in on your turn, and you have one riff you can repeat in whatever key the guitars call.
3, middle
You can play blues phrases in the guitar keys, add background lines and stabs behind a singer, and trade fours.
4 to 5, experienced
You know the melodies of the common shuffles, you can harmonize a background line with another horn on the spot, and you can pace a slow-blues solo without rushing it.

Inquire about a good teacher for you, and I'll recommend someone if I know one.

Harmonica players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play in second position on a couple of harps, say a C and an A, you know the twelve-bar form, and you stay out while the singer sings.
3, middle
Your bends hit the pitch, you can play train rhythms and chugging patterns, you can cup a mic, and you can build a solo over one time through the form.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play third position for a minor blues, keep a good tone at low volume, play amplified or acoustic, and trade solos with a guitarist without turning it into a volume contest.

Inquire about a good teacher for you, and I'll recommend someone if I know one.
