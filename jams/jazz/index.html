Jams · Jazz

How jazz jams work

Jazz jams run on a shared songbook. A player calls a standard by name and key, and a band of people who may have just met plays it.

What happens on the night

What gets played

Players call standards, including blues tunes, rhythm changes, and songs like Autumn Leaves, All of Me, Blue Bossa and Summertime. Hosts tend to keep the early sets to the best-known tunes so more players can join.

How signup works

Signup is often looser than at a blues jam. Some nights keep a list, and others run on a word with the host. Some nights are sit-ins with a house trio and have no open list, so find out which kind of night it is before you go.

How a set goes

A tune starts with the melody, called the head, then players take solos over the form, sometimes trade fours with the drummer, and play the head again to end. Whoever called the tune sets the key, the tempo and the feel, and everyone else accompanies.

Habits worth knowing

  • Call tunes you can play without a chart. If you need the page, pick an easier tune.
  • Comp lightly while someone else solos, and sometimes drop out entirely.
  • Bass players and drummers often play whole sets, so pace yourselves.
  • Make the end of your solo obvious so the next player knows where the band is in the form.

What each instrumentalist needs

Find your instrument, then the level you want to play at.

  • 1 to 2, first-timer friendly Your first time on any stage is fine, and so is playing basic chords or a groove and following along.
  • 3, middle You hold your part, take a solo or a verse, and know the common tunes.
  • 4 to 5, experienced People expect you to know the tunes by name and keep up, and working pros sit in.

All five levels, defined

Guitarists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play shell voicings through a blues in F and B-flat, comp a steady quarter-note rhythm, and keep your place in the melody of one standard.
3, middle
You know two-five-one phrases, you comp without getting in the piano's way, you can play a dozen standards from memory, and you can trade fours.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play rhythm changes and a minor blues at tempo, play an intro or an ending on request, and share the comping with a pianist by ear.

We build a working set of standards and practice comping the way a rhythm section wants it. Guitar lessons

Bassists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play roots and fifths in two through a blues in F, and follow the drummer's feel without rushing.
3, middle
You can walk through standard chord changes, open in two and move into four, play the common turnarounds, and keep the form when a soloist stretches over it.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play any standard at any tempo, use pedal points and substitutions on the fly, trade fours, and keep the form steady for everyone else.

We learn walking lines and the standards that get called, and we practice keeping the form under pressure. Bass lessons

Drummers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can keep a steady ride pattern, play in two and in four, and keep your volume under the soloist.
3, middle
You can swing at medium and fast tempos, play a bossa and a straight-eighth feel, trade fours, and catch the hits in a melody you half know.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play fast tempos without tension and ballads with brushes, play Latin grooves, and shape the whole band's dynamics from the kit.

We work on swing feel at real tempos and practice trading fours and catching cues. Drum lessons

Piano and keyboard players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play shell voicings for a blues in F and a few two-five-ones, and comp softly in time.
3, middle
You can play rootless voicings, you know a dozen standards, you can build an intro from the last eight bars, and you can comp behind a soloist without crowding them.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play any standard that gets called, start a tune with a solo intro, set a ballad on request, and feed the changes to a lost soloist without stopping the tune.

We build rootless voicings and a list of tunes you can call, and we practice comping for people you've never played with. Piano lessons

Singers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You know one standard in your key with the melody solid, and you have that key written down to hand the pianist.
3, middle
You have a few standards with clear keys and forms, you count off your own tempo, and you come back in at the top after the solos.
4 to 5, experienced
You have a book of tunes with keys, you cue endings and tags, and you can front a band of strangers like it's your own gig.

Horn players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play a blues in concert F and B-flat, you know the melody of one standard, and you count your rests so you come in on your turn.
3, middle
You know a dozen melodies from memory, you have two-five-one phrases in the common keys, you can play backgrounds behind a singer, and you can trade fours.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play melodies at tempo in the common keys, double a melody in octaves or harmony on the spot, and pace a solo past one time through the form.
