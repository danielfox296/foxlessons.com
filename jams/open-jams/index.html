Jams · Open jams

How open jams work

Open jams take any player and any genre. The host builds bands from whoever signed up, so the music changes with who shows up each week.

What happens on the night

What gets played

Bands play whatever the people on stage can agree on, like a blues shuffle, four-chord rock, a funk vamp, a country two-step, or a song somebody brought. Hosts steer groups toward tunes everyone on stage can fake.

How signup works

Open jams almost always use a list where you write your name and your instrument. The host mixes groups so each one has drums, bass and someone on chords, then works down the list in rounds.

How a set goes

Each group plays two or three tunes. With the host's help, the group agrees on something simple in a few seconds, and someone counts it in.

Habits worth knowing

  • Name everything you can play when you sign up, so the host can build a group that works.
  • Bring the song you'd call, key included, so the huddle on stage stays short.
  • Pick tunes the least experienced player on stage can play.
  • Switch instruments only if the host offers, since the list sets the order.

What each instrumentalist needs

Find your instrument, then the level you want to play at.

  • 1 to 2, first-timer friendly Your first time on any stage is fine, and so is playing basic chords or a groove and following along.
  • 3, middle You hold your part, take a solo or a verse, and know the common tunes.
  • 4 to 5, experienced People expect you to know the tunes by name and keep up, and working pros sit in.

All five levels, defined

Guitarists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You know a dozen open chords, you have a capo, and you can follow a called key when someone says the chord names out loud.
3, middle
You can play barre chords in any key, follow the common progressions by number, and take a short pentatonic solo.
4 to 5, experienced
You can hear chord changes without being told, cover bass or lead when the group needs it, and arrange a stranger's song on the spot.

We make you the guitarist a pickup band is glad to see, with keys, numbers and steady time. Guitar lessons

Bassists

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play roots on beat one with your eyes on the guitarist's hands, and you ask for the key before the count-in.
3, middle
You can hear one-four-five and the common pop progressions, walk between chords, and lock in with a drummer you just met.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play any style the list throws at you, fill whatever gaps the group has, and run the band from the back without saying much.

We train your ear for progressions and for locking in with a drummer you don't know. Bass lessons

Drummers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play one steady rock beat and one shuffle, at the band's tempo.
3, middle
You can match a feel to a song you've never heard, play brushes on the soft ones, and end songs off eye contact.
4 to 5, experienced
You can play country, funk, swing and Latin feels on demand, steady a rushing band, and count in at the right tempo for the song.

We build the common feels and practice listening before you play. Drum lessons

Keyboard players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play triads from chord names called out loud, and play softly until you're sure.
3, middle
You can play by numbers and keys, choose between pads and comping, and fill only the space the band leaves.
4 to 5, experienced
You can cover the bass with your left hand when there's no bassist, play an intro when asked, and keep a mixed group together from the keyboard.

We work on reading chord charts, the number system, and playing less. Piano lessons

Singers

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You know one song with the words down and your key on paper, and you're patient while the band finds it.
3, middle
You have a few songs in different feels, you can give the band the progression in numbers, and you can start on pitch from the guitarist's chord.
4 to 5, experienced
You can lead the group by calling the song, the key, the feel and the ending, and you can put a band of strangers at ease.

Inquire about a good teacher for you, and I'll recommend someone if I know one.

Horn players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You can play long tones under the band, you know the melody of one tune, and you ask which concert key has been called.
3, middle
You can play riffs and pads by ear in the common keys, take a solo over a twelve-bar blues, and sit out for whole verses.
4 to 5, experienced
You can fake melodies by ear, harmonize a line with another horn, and add intros, stops and endings the group didn't plan.

Inquire about a good teacher for you, and I'll recommend someone if I know one.

Harmonica players

1 to 2, first-timer friendly
You have a C harp and an A harp, you know second-position basics, and you play only in the gaps.
3, middle
You can match a harp to the called key fast, play folk and blues fills, and lock chugging rhythms with the drummer.
4 to 5, experienced
You have a full set of harps, you know your positions, you can play the melody when there's no singer, and you know when to stop playing.

Inquire about a good teacher for you, and I'll recommend someone if I know one.
