Music apps versus a teacher: what each actually does.

Yousician, Simply Piano, Fender Play, YouTube. I recommend some of them, so this isn't a takedown.

The verdict in two sentences.

An app is the best twenty dollars a month a beginner can spend on daily practice, and I tell my own students to keep theirs. What an app can't do is see you, and that gap, posture, wrists, tone, feel, is exactly where most self-taught progress stalls.

Their offer, fairly

What the apps are genuinely good at.

As of August 2026: Yousician and Fender Play run $19.99 a month, Simply Piano about $15 to $18 a month, and YouTube is free plus your patience. The good ones turn daily practice into a streak you don't want to break, give you instant note-by-note feedback, and put a structured path in front of you at any hour. That's real value, and it's why pairing an app with lessons works better than either alone.

I learned this gap personally, before apps existed. I spent my school years playing drums along with headphones, the Led Zeppelin box set, Abbey Road, hours after school. It felt wonderful, and it built real things. But with a wonderfully produced band in your ears, you're captured by the emotion of it, and you never hear your own shortcomings in technique and expression. The app version of that is the green checkmark: right notes, wrong hands, and nobody there to say so.

The honest fork

Choose an app if. Add a teacher if.

Stay app-only if budget is the deciding constraint, if you're testing whether the itch is real, or if daily gamified reps are all you want from music right now. That's a legitimate place to be.

Add a teacher if your hands hurt or your sound disappoints you, if you've plateaued where the app can't tell you why, if you want to get off the app's catalog and onto your own songs by ear and chord chart, or if the point of music, for you, includes another human hearing you play.
