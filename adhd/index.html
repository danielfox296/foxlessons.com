Music lessons for ADHD brains.

Interest first, movement built in, ear before paper, and zero shame about the blank week. You don't need a diagnosis to want lessons that work this way.

Book your first lesson

If lessons didn't stick before, look at the method first.

The standard lesson method fails a lot of ADHD people the same way: assigned songs you don't care about, minute quotas, notation before music, and a weekly serving of shame when the practice log comes up empty. You got handed a method built on obligation when your attention runs on interest. When it didn't stick, everyone assumed the problem was you.

Here's how lessons with me run instead. This isn't an ADHD add-on to some other curriculum. It's how I teach, period.

How lessons run

The commitments.

Interest first, immediately.

The whole point of music is to make something enjoyable and to enjoy doing it. That starts with wherever you're at and whatever you're into. We work on real songs you're actually interested in, from the start. If exercises are needed, for improvisation or to get more facility around the instrument, we use them, but only as far as they serve the songs you want to play.

We follow the energy.

We can switch things around. We can switch songs. I'll only push on something if you're close to a breakthrough. Music is physical, physiological, and mental, so we use the body, the ears, and the mind, and switching between them is how we reset the room when you need a reset.

Ear and patterns, not paper.

Reading music is not part of what we do. We work by ear and from chord charts. You'll learn to identify what's happening in a song and play along with it, without worrying about how it's notated.

A feeling, not minutes.

I don't set minute quotas or daily floors. We go for a feeling. If you get the lick down in five minutes instead of twenty, you're done.

The blank week gets problem-solving, not shame.

Shame is a heavy tactic, and I'm not going to use it. If a week went by and the instrument stayed untouched, we problem-solve instead: is the goal connected to your actual life, and what's in the way? I want to help you get in touch with what you really care about, so you can build your practice around something you actually want. That's coach work, and I lean into it.

The instrument lives where you live.

If touching the instrument is the hard part, we figure out what pulls you toward it and what you're afraid of. Often the fix is physical. Put the instrument in the middle of the room, where you actually sit. Out of the case, in your way. A lot of times that alone does it.

Feedback matches your goal.

I'm not light on correction. The feedback matches what you've told me you're committed to. In it for fun? Feedback stays light. Working toward something specific and time-bound? I'll coach you hard toward it.

The room works for your senses.

Lights, volume, comfort: I'm sensitive to sensory needs, ADHD and AuDHD both, and we set the room up so it supports you. In your own home, that's easy.

Between lessons, you can reach me.

Midweek, if you want it, I check in by text or a call. Did you get to it, where did it snag, and quick answers on the tune if I know it off the top of my head. No instrument in hand, no video, no homework theater. Founding students also get direct text access to me between lessons.

The science, honestly

The honest version of the evidence.

Music training has reasonable evidence behind it for attention and inhibition skills in general child populations. The ADHD-specific studies, especially around rhythm and drumming, are promising and small. And nobody disputes that interest-driven, movement-rich, immediate-feedback activity is the kind of thing ADHD attention engages with. So here's where I land: I can't promise music will change your brain. I can promise lessons built the way your attention actually engages. Interest first, movement, patterns before paper, quick wins.

And to say it plainly: I'm a music teacher, not a music therapist. My job is helping you play. If you're looking for clinical goals with music, Denver has board-certified music therapists, and I'm glad to point you toward them.

For parents

ADHD kids, same commitments.

Everything above holds for kids: their music, their pace, movement between segments, no minute quotas, no shame. Lessons run 45 minutes in your home with a parent home, which also means no waiting room, no fluorescent studio, and a familiar room with their own stuff in it.

You don't need a diagnosis or paperwork to ask for lessons that run this way. Tell me what helps your kid and what words your family uses, and those are the words we'll use.
